# Legacy guide: economics and finance storytelling for Mộc Vy

This guide is retained for existing Mộc Vy episode continuity only. New Status & Systems documentaries use that active profile's human-experience/institution format and the channel documentary skill. New Vì Sao Nơi Này episodes use place-led geography questions. Do not revive mission defaults or individualized financial advice from legacy material.

For legacy Mộc Vy episodes about economics, household finance, public policy, prices, income, work, and financial systems, keep the voice conversational and story-led. Explain what the evidence means without turning a feeling into an unsupported universal claim or giving individualized investment advice. Do not use this as the default path for new episodes.

## Start with a question, not a verdict

Begin with a familiar tension, observation, or question a viewer can recognize. Treat the proposed explanation as a hypothesis to investigate. For example, ask why economic growth can coexist with financial pressure for some households; do not start by declaring that everyone is poorer or that wages never rise.

Use a concrete anchor: a bill, a grocery basket, a rent renewal, a paycheck, a loan payment, a small business decision, or a documented household survey. A hypothetical example must be introduced as hypothetical. Do not invent a real person's quote, income, debt, or hardship and present it as reporting.

## Make a story card and claim ledger

For each episode, record:

- **Viewer question:** the single everyday question the episode answers.
- **Promise:** what the viewer will understand by the end.
- **Anchor:** the transaction, budget, policy, business, or household evidence that makes the topic concrete.
- **Working answer:** a provisional explanation that can change if the evidence contradicts it.
- **Mechanism:** how the policy or economic process connects to the outcome.
- **Who may experience it differently:** groups, regions, sectors, or household types the evidence distinguishes.
- **Scope and limit:** what the episode cannot conclude.

Keep a claim ledger with each statistic's source, release date, period, geography, population, unit, definition, and caveat. Prefer official statistical releases and methodology notes, central-bank or ministry publications where relevant, audited records, and high-quality research. YouTube scans may help find questions and formats; they do not validate economic claims or audience retention.

## Keep unlike measures separate

Before comparing two numbers, confirm that they refer to compatible periods, populations, units, and definitions. Explain the distinctions that matter to the episode:

- Real GDP growth measures a change in total output after price adjustment; it is not the percentage change in each person's pay.
- Inflation is the rate of price change; a lower inflation rate does not mean the general price level has returned to an earlier level.
- CPI tracks a representative basket; a household's actual costs can differ by location, housing status, dependents, and spending pattern.
- Average income can move differently from median income, a particular worker's wage, disposable income, or household cash flow.
- Nominal income and inflation-adjusted purchasing power are different measures. Do not subtract mismatched rates or periods and present the result as an individual's real pay change.
- GDP growth, asset prices, household wealth, debt, and monthly liquidity answer different questions.

Do not imply that a statistic proves a causal mechanism unless the source or research design supports that link. Separate correlation, interpretation, and established cause.

## Use a flexible spoken arc

1. **Open:** a relatable question or moment, followed quickly by the episode's promise.
2. **Reveal:** introduce one surprising, well-sourced number or fact and explain what it measures.
3. **Unpack:** follow the mechanism in plain language, one step at a time.
4. **Complicate:** show why averages, regions, sectors, or household situations differ, using evidence rather than assumptions.
5. **Answer:** return to the opening question, state what the data supports, name what it cannot settle, and leave a useful way to think about the issue.

This is a flexible guide, not a formula. Write a continuous narration that sounds like Mộc Vy talking with a viewer. Use short questions sparingly, avoid stacked rhetorical questions, and introduce technical terms only when they help answer the central question. Do not begin with a list of definitions or a parade of statistics.

## Editorial and financial boundaries

- Do not frame a conclusion first and select only the numbers that confirm it.
- Do not say “everyone is poorer,” “wages are flat,” or “prices all rose” unless the evidence supports that exact scope and period.
- Show whose experience an average represents and whose experience it may hide.
- Keep examples and financial scenarios transparent; distinguish educational explanation from personalized financial, tax, or investment advice.
- Avoid promising a simple fix to structural or policy problems.
- Respect people facing debt, unemployment, poverty, or financial stress. Humor may clarify a situation but must not target them.

## Review before approval

Use the shared story-edit and retention audits. In the story edit, verify that the question, promise, and answer align; check every figure and causal claim; and listen or read aloud for natural Vietnamese cadence. In the retention audit, make the promise clear early, introduce a useful clue in the opening, and ensure every section changes the viewer's understanding rather than repeating the same complaint. Report factual and methodological limits separately from editorial scores.
