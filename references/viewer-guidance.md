# Viewer guidance through curiosity and comprehension

Revision: documentary-viewer-guidance-v1 (17/10/2026). This is an active shared layer for all six channels. It uses cognitive psychology as a design aid and YouTube Analytics as post-publication feedback. It does not claim that a script can guarantee completion or recommendation.

## What the evidence supports

Curiosity can arise when a viewer recognizes a meaningful gap between what is known and what they want to understand. The information-gap account is a theoretical review, not a universal law of video retention. Experiments found that curiosity can motivate information seeking and improve memory for sought information and, in some cases, incidental information. Those studies do not show that cliffhangers or withholding a known answer make YouTube viewers finish a long video.

Viewers also organize continuous activity into meaningful events and update their understanding when the situation changes. Research on narrative reading and film supports useful boundaries around changes in time, place, goal, action or state. Experiments with narrated explanatory animation found that learner control over pacing could improve transfer in a specific lightning lesson, without improving simple retention scores. These findings support clear explanatory steps and meaningful transitions; they do not establish a universal cut, chapter or re-hook interval.

YouTube defines the intro metric as the share still watching after the first 30 seconds and recommends checking whether that section fulfills the title and thumbnail expectation. The retention graph marks skips, exits, rewatches and shares, but a spike can also indicate confusion. Use the actual video-level data to form hypotheses and inspect the corresponding narration and visuals; do not treat a text score or graph shape as proof of cause.

Curiosity can change as evidence unfolds, rather than staying at one level until a final reveal. In a controlled line-drawing task, participants who reported more curiosity were more willing to keep watching instead of taking an immediate spoiler. This supports progressive, meaningful evidence as a hypothesis for writing; it does not show that delaying a known answer improves long-form YouTube retention. A study of short narrative videos found that shared attention patterns predicted time viewers were willing to allocate, and compared its engagement measure with StoryCorps YouTube viewing data. Its stimuli were short and its findings do not establish a formula for 14–30-minute Vietnamese explainers. Treat both as useful evidence with narrow transfer, not as universal retention laws.

Tension theory offers a cautious explanation for why a real constraint or unresolved outcome can matter: conflict, instability or uncertainty can prompt viewers to predict what follows and want resolution. The authors present a theoretical model, not a tested script recipe. Use only documentary uncertainty that exists in the evidence, and let each passage change what the viewer can predict.

## Apply it while writing

1. **Set an accurate expectation.** Define the viewer's central question from the evidence and the live channel format. Make the title, thumbnail, opening image and first spoken line point to the same question or demonstrated outcome. Do not imply a result, comparison or danger that the episode cannot support.
2. **Make the gap understandable.** Give enough place, time, task or process context for a new viewer to see what is unexpected and why the answer matters. Within roughly the first 30 seconds, confirm the click and provide a real piece of orientation, evidence or answer. Thirty seconds is a useful analytics checkpoint, not a required hook length.
3. **Guide a chain of understanding.** For each major passage, record the viewer's active question, the source-backed observation or change, what the viewer can now explain, and the next question or dependency that genuinely follows. Pay smaller questions when the evidence allows. Keep an unresolved question only while the episode advances it; do not tease a fact already known or create a cliffhanger from missing evidence.
4. **Mark real event boundaries.** Orient the viewer when time, place, actor, task, physical state or explanatory model changes. Let one passage establish a usable relationship before adding another mechanism or a cluster of numbers. Use technical terms after the job or constraint they name is clear. Retain dates, definitions and uncertainty when they alter the conclusion.
5. **Make image and narration support the same model.** A still, map, comparison or cross-section should clarify the current state or show a sourced change. Hold a useful diagram while its relationship is being explained; change the image when the evidence, state, scale or task changes. Camera movement directs attention but cannot stand in for a new event. Avoid arbitrary pattern interrupts and redundant text.
6. **Pay the promise once.** Close the central question with the supported answer, relevant consequence and material limit. A callback or next-video question is optional and comes after the payoff. A viewer's understanding and satisfaction matter more than keeping every loop open until the last line.

## Apply it while reviewing

- Read the title and thumbnail as a new viewer, then inspect the first image and first 30 seconds of approved narration. Does each confirm the same truthful promise? What useful progress has occurred by the 30-second point?
- Map the major passage sequence: active question → evidence/change → updated understanding → next dependency or payoff. Mark where each question is answered. Flag passages that repeat information, introduce an unrelated branch, or ask the viewer to remember details without a later use.
- Check cognitive load: can a new viewer explain the current task and the one relationship the passage adds? Do names, dates or numbers arrive before their purpose is clear? Does the image clarify the spoken explanation?
- Inspect the middle for a genuine change in understanding, not a mandatory percentage-based re-hook. If the middle stalls, repair the missing evidence, repeated explanation, unclear relation or irrelevant branch.
- After publication, inspect the 30-second intro, dips, spikes and top moments alongside the exact script/image, video length and traffic source. Compare similar videos when available. Spikes require review because they may reflect interest, sharing or confusion. Record hypotheses separately from observed audience data.

## Evidence and limits

- Loewenstein, G. (1994). “The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation.” *Psychological Bulletin*, 116(1), 75–98. DOI: [10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75). The information-gap account describes one route into curiosity and also discusses its intensity, transience and possible disappointment when satisfied.
- Kang, M. J. et al. (2009). “The Wick in the Candle of Learning: Epistemic Curiosity Activates Reward Circuitry and Enhances Memory.” *Psychological Science*, 20(8). DOI: [10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x). Laboratory trivia tasks linked higher curiosity with information seeking; this is not a YouTube completion study.
- Hsiung, A. et al. (2023). “Curiosity evolves as information unfolds.” *PNAS*, 120(43), e2301974120. [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10614840/). In an evolving-drawing task, curiosity varied as participants received information; higher curiosity predicted patience to continue rather than immediately view a spoiler. It did not test documentary scripts or long-form YouTube.
- Cohen, S. S., Henin, S. & Parra, L. C. (2017). “Engaging narratives evoke similar neural activity and lead to similar time perception.” *Scientific Reports*, 7, 4578. [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5496904/). Attention synchronization in short narrative videos predicted time participants were willing to commit; the analysis also used StoryCorps viewing data. The short stimuli and context limit transfer to our episodes.
- Lehne, M. & Koelsch, S. (2015). “Toward a general psychological model of tension and suspense.” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 6, 79. [Full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00079/full). A theoretical model connects tension with conflict, instability, uncertainty and prediction; it is not a validated retention formula.
- Gruber, M. J., Gelman, B. D. & Ranganath, C. (2014). “States of Curiosity Modulate Hippocampus-Dependent Learning via the Dopaminergic Circuit.” *Neuron*, 84(2), 486–496. [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/). Curiosity in the study improved immediate and delayed memory for queried and some incidental information; it did not test long-form viewing completion.
- Zacks, J. M., Speer, N. K. & Reynolds, J. R. (2009). “Segmentation in Reading and Film Comprehension.” *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 138(2), 307–327. [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2263140/). Narrative situation changes predicted event boundaries and influenced processing; this supports meaningful transitions, not fixed cuts.
- Mayer, R. E. & Chandler, P. (2001). “When Learning Is Just a Click Away: Does Simple User Interaction Foster Deeper Understanding of Multimedia Messages?” *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 93(2), 390–397. DOI: [10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.390](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.390). The narrated-animation experiments concerned learning and transfer from a lightning explanation, not YouTube retention.
- YouTube Help, [Measure key moments for audience retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415) and [Understand your content performance for YouTube's recommendation system](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650?hl=en). Platform guidance identifies appeal, engagement and satisfaction, and recommends inspecting actual retention against the title/thumbnail promise.

The external [`storytelling-hooks`](https://github.com/yaxeen/storytelling-skills/tree/main/skills/storytelling-hooks) and [`long-form-youtube`](https://github.com/yaxeen/storytelling-skills/tree/main/skills/long-form-youtube) GitHub skills are practitioner templates, not empirical validation. The first is mainly for short-form hooks; the second targets roughly 6–25-minute videos. Adapt their accurate-promise, clear-opening, grounded tension, useful pacing variation and explicit payoff checks to the selected documentary channel. Treat the six levers as questions for diagnosing a draft, not ingredients that every episode must contain.

Do not adopt their fixed loop chains, chapter counts, cliffhangers, audience “private worry” prompts, compulsory CTAs, two-second long-form hook target or 20–40-second/40–60-percent timing prescriptions. The source skill's recommendation to hold a loop until the end also does not override our rule to pay partial answers as evidence allows. These patterns do not establish that a script will retain viewers; only published analytics can describe observed audience behavior.
