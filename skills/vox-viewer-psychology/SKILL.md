# Viewer psychology for documentary scripts

Use this specialist with `../vox-channel-documentary/SKILL.md`, the live channel profile and `../../references/viewer-guidance.md`. It guides attention and comprehension; it does not replace the channel's subject, story format, factual standards or voice.

## North star

Treat a viewer as a person deciding whether the next minute is worth their limited time. Respect the title/thumbnail expectation, give useful progress, make each next step understandable, and fulfill the promise. Optimize the experience and value of watching, not watch time at any cost. No script can guarantee completion or recommendation.

## Workflow

1. **Model the viewer's starting point.** Write what a new viewer saw in the title/thumbnail, what they likely know already, what is unclear, and why the answer could matter. Do not assume specialist knowledge, a personal worry, or a real person's private thoughts.
2. **Create a bounded information gap.** State one accurate central question and answerable subquestions. Give enough place, time, task and evidence for the viewer to recognize the gap and trust that the episode can answer it. Confirm the promise in the first image and spoken line; by roughly 0:30, provide orientation and a useful verified advance. Thirty seconds is a YouTube analytics checkpoint, not a mandatory hook formula.
3. **Map the viewer's gains.** For each major passage, record: active viewer question; sourced evidence or state change; what the viewer can now explain, distinguish or predict; the next genuinely unresolved question/dependency or payoff. Let evidence change the viewer's model over time. Pay partial answers when supported; do not repeat teasers or hold back an answer already available.
4. **Earn the next minute.** At each passage boundary, ask: what did the viewer receive, what remains unclear for a real reason, and why is the next passage the useful step toward resolving it? If the next passage adds only another study name, term, number or paraphrase, merge it or cut it. A transition may be causal consequence, prerequisite or next task; do not turn one into another without evidence.
5. **Manage effort and orientation.** Establish place, time, task and current state before adding a new relationship. Explain a mechanism's practical job before its technical term. Introduce numbers when their scale, threshold or comparison has a purpose. Usually add one major causal relation at a time; keep dates and uncertainty when they change the answer. At real changes of time, place, actor, task, state or model, reorient rather than making viewers reconstruct context from memory.
6. **Use images to lower effort.** A map, still, comparison or cross-section should show the same relationship currently spoken. Keep a diagram long enough to understand it; change it when evidence, state, scale or task changes. Camera motion, captions and pattern interrupts cannot repair unclear explanation.
7. **Build tension only from the evidence.** Tension can come from a documented conflict, unstable system, constrained choice or uncertain consequential outcome. Identify what is known, what remains uncertain and what evidence can resolve it. Do not invent crises, emotional stakes, a protagonist, danger or a fixed cliffhanger/re-hook schedule. Questions are useful only when the next evidence changes what the viewer can know.
8. **Close the exchange.** Answer the central promise once with the supported consequence and material limit. A callback, future question or discussion prompt is optional and belongs after the answer. Do not stack summaries or end with a new teaser that weakens the payoff.
9. **Review honestly.** Run separate story-edit and retention passes on the full current speech, record its source and normalized SHA-256, quote the opening, consequential middle, ending and longest stalled stretch, and verify every claimed edit against the text. Label text-only and same-author reviews. After publication, compare actual video-level retention with the exact words, visuals, video length and traffic source; treat dips/spikes as signals to investigate, not causes by themselves.

## Optional storytelling pattern toolkit

For long-form episodes, adapt the useful checks from the [long-form-youtube skill](https://github.com/yaxeen/storytelling-skills/tree/main/skills/long-form-youtube): lock the viewer promise across title/thumbnail/opening, start without throat-clearing, make the question answerable, vary information density when the material permits, and explicitly pay off the promise. Borrow the six-lever vocabulary from [storytelling-hooks](https://github.com/yaxeen/storytelling-skills/tree/main/skills/storytelling-hooks) as diagnostic questions: is there a real information gap, an evidenced conflict, a grounded human consequence, a meaningful expectation change, and a complete resolution?

These are practitioner checklists, not evidence that a formula raises retention. `storytelling-hooks` is aimed mainly at short-form openings; do not import its two-second target, audience-qualification language or three-layer Shorts packaging as long-form rules. From `long-form-youtube`, do not require a fixed chapter count, loop chain, cliffhanger, percentage-based sag-point re-hook, 20–40-second pattern interrupt, private-worry avatar or mandatory CTA. Let the live channel DNA and sourced events determine passage boundaries, emotion and pacing. Close a question when the evidence supports it, even if another question remains for a legitimate reason.

## Evidence limits

Read `../../references/viewer-guidance.md` for the sourced research and limits. Laboratory curiosity findings, narrative-engagement studies, event-segmentation research and multimedia-learning experiments can inform hypotheses about attention and comprehension. They do not establish a retention formula for our 12–20-minute episodes. Only the episode's published audience analytics can describe its measured retention; editorial scores, TTS impressions and public view counts are not substitutes.
